# 1. Summary of application 

# 1.1  Executive summary 

1.1.1  This application seeks retrospective full planning permission for the temporary use of part of 

Clapham Common for modular sauna s and associated infrastructure , located within the splash 

pad water play area . The proposed dates for this use are between 1st October 2025 and 30 th 

April 2026 . It is noted that the use has commenced on site prior to permission being granted ,

therefore, the description of development now reads as being retrospective .

1.1.2  The proposed temporary use is for a leisure facility comprising two sauna cabins, six plunge 

tubs, a small kiosk for staff operations and customer transactions, a seating area covered by a 

stretch tent canopy, and 2 -metre -high Heras fencing with a wood -eff ect finish and plywood 

entrance gates. The majority of the structures are positioned on existing hardstanding adjacent 

to the splash pool facility within Clapham Common. 

1.1.3  Officers consider that the principle of the temporary use of this small section of Clapham 

Common for the installation of sauna facilities and associated infrastructure is acceptable and 

represents an appropriate form of development within Metropolitan Open Land ( “MOL ”). The 

proposal has been considered to have low levels of less than substantial harm to the 

conservation area and listed building, Church of Holy Trinity . This harm has been carefully 

considered and weighed against the identified public benefits of the scheme, in accordance 

with the statutory requirements and rele vant planning guidance. On balance, the benefits are 

judged to outweigh the harm. The associated impacts in respect of transport, residential 

amenity, biodiversity, trees, ecology, flooding, sustainability, and public safety have been fully 

assessed and are considered acceptable. 

1.1.4  When the development plan is looked at as a whole, in officers’ assessment , the proposal 

accords with it. In officers’ assessment, the identified conflict with heritage policies is not such 

as to mean that the development plan, when looked at overall, is not complied with. The 

application is therefore recommended for approval, su bject to conditions, in accordance with 

the presumption in favour of sustainable development conferred upon local planning 

authorities by the National Planning Policy Framework 2024 (“NPPF ”).

# 2. Site c ontext and proposal 

# 2.1  Site description 

2.1.1  Clapham Common is a large public open space, measuring approximately 206 acres , located 

between the London Boroughs of Lambeth and Wandsworth. The Common is designated as 

MOL and lies within the Clapham Conservation Area. It is a historically significant landscape 

that has served as a public amenity since the 19th century. 

2.1.2  Clapham Common is bounded by Clapham Common North Side and South Side, with The 

Pavement and Long Road forming part of its eastern edge. The application site is situated 

adjacent to the former Cock Pond area, now redeveloped as a splashpad water play facil ity, 

near the Holy Trinity Church and Clapham Old Town. 

Image 1: The site outlined in red in relation to the wider Common 

2.1.3  Clapham Common is a designated Conservation Area (CA) and contains several listed 

buildings and structures, including Holy Trinity Church (Grade II*), the Clapham Common War 

Memorial (Grade II), and the historic drinking fountain (Grade II). The Common has a rich 

history of recreational use, with features such as the former Cock Pond, now adapted for 

modern play facilitie s. 

2.1.4  The Common accommodates a variety of leisure and recreational facilities. The former Cock 

Pond area, where the proposal is located, has historically been associated with water -based 

recreation and was recently developed through the approved splashpad development and 

associated infrastructure. ↑ To contents 

2.1.5  Clapham Common has a long tradition of hosting community events and activities, contributing 

to its communal and cultural significance. It remains publicly accessible throughout the year 

and is a focal point for local residents and visitors. 

2.1.6  The application site is a small, hard -surfaced area of the splashpad facility, enclosed by low 

railings and accessed via existing gates. It is positioned away from residential properties .

2.1.7  The application site does not contain any listed buildings but is within Clapham Conservation 

Area.

4.2.2  Principle of land use 

Presumption in favour of sustainable development 

4.2.2.1  The NPPF sets the presumption in favour of sustainable development, and this is reflected in 

LLP Policy D2 and the other policies in the plan. LLP Policy D2 states that the Council will 

always work proactively with applicants jointly to find solutions which mean that proposals can 

be approved wherever possible and to secure development that improves the economic, social 

and environmental conditions in Lambeth. 

Proposed temporary use 

4.2.2.2  Clapham Common is a major public open space bounded by main roads and mature trees, 

with a long history of sports, recreation, cultural activities, and community events. It is 

designated as MOL and lies within the [Clapham Common Conservation Area ], which includes 

several listed buildings such as Holy Trinity Church (Grade II*). The Common is heavily used 

for leisure and recreation .↑ To contents 

4.2.2.3  LLP Policy PN5 outlines the importance of conserving, managing and improving Clapham 

Common through the implementation of the Clapham Common Masterplan. 

4.2.2.4  The principle of temporary recreational use aligns with LLP Policy S2 , EN1 and PN 5, provided 

the nature of the activity and its impact on openness are acceptable. 

4.2.2.5  Policy G3 of the LP relates to MOL and seeks to give the strongest protection to MOL from 

inappropriate development; giving it the same level of protection that is offered to the Green 

Belt. Development should be limited to small scale structures to suppor t outdoor open space 

uses and minimise any adverse impact on the openness of MOL. 

4.2.2.6  The proposal would not result in any permanent loss of existing public open space; the open 

space will be returned to its existing situation and there are no permanent alterations 

proposed .

4.2.2.7  When considering an application involving MOL, reference to the ‘Green Belt’ needs to be 

read as reference to MOL. Paragraphs 1 42 -160 of the NPPF relate to protecting Green Belt 

land. Paragraph 1 42 of the NPPF states that “the Government attaches great importance to 

Green Belts. The fundamental aim of Green Belt policy is to prevent urban sprawl by keeping 

land permanently open; the essential characteristics of Green Belts are their openness and 

their permanence”. 

4.2.2.8  Considering the aims set out above, the proposal would not result in urban sprawl and would 

not affect the permanence of the Green Belt . Due to the size , location and temporary nature, 

the Common would remain without any permanent alteration and the areas where the 

temporary use is located will revert back to open space. While the installation introduces 

additional built form and enclosure, its modest scale, seasonal nature, and location within an 

established fenced area limit visual and spatial impact. Vi ews across the Common will largely 

remain open, and the proposal does not conflict with the purposes of MOL as set out in NPPF 

paragraphs 142 –143. The development supports outdoor recreation and community wellbeing, 

consistent with NPPF paragraph 154(b), w hich allows appropriate facilities for sport and 

recreation provided openness is preserved. There would be no engineering operations as a 

result of the proposal. There would be no long term displacement of natural vegetation or 

habitat .

4.2.2.9  Paragraph 15 3 of the NPPF state s that local planning authorities should ensure that 

substantial weight is given to any harm to the Green Belt, including harm to its openness and 

that inappropriate development in Green Belt ‘should not be approved except in very special 

circumstances.’ ‘Very special circumstances’ will not exist unless the potential harm to the 

Green Belt by reason of inappropriateness, and any other harm resulting from the proposal, is 

clearly outweighed by other considerations. 

4.2.2.10  Development in the Green Belt is inappropriate unless one of the exceptions detailed within 

NPPF Paragraph 154 applies . For this proposal , it is considered that the exception in part b is 

most relevant: 

b) the provision of appropriate facilities (in connection with the existing use of land or a change 

of use), including buildings, for outdoor sport, outdoor recreation, cemeteries and burial 

grounds and allotments; as long as the facilities preserve the op enness of the Green Belt and 

do not conflict with the purposes of including land within it; ↑ To contents 

4.2.2.11  Considering paragraph 154(b) of the NPPF, the proposal is seasonal, low -impact, and 

enhances the recreational and cultural use of Clapham Common. It activates an underused 

space during winter months due to the splashpark not being open supports community 

wellbeing, and expands the visitor options to the area and Common . The area is modest in 

scale, involves no permanent structures, and will be fully restored at the end of the operating 

period. Officers consider this use consistent with the interpretation of “outdoor recreation,” 

which includes temporary leisure facilities. Therefore, the proposal is considered a compatible 

and acceptable temporary use within MOL, contributing positively to the Common’s role as a 

community asset and meeting the exception unde r paragraph 154(b). 

4.2.2.12  Notwithstanding the above, paragraph 154(b) allows for certain operations and buildings 

while preserving openness. In this case, aside from the fencing and small kiosk, the main site 

area will remain largely open, with sauna cabins and plunge pools positio ned on existing 

hardstanding. While some fencing and structures may partially reduce views, these elements

the park’s edge —ensures that wider views across the Commo n remain open and 

uninterrupted. Assessing the development holistically, officers consider that the limited spatial 

and visual changes would preserve the openness of the MOL. 

4.2.2.13  Having regard to the five purposes of the Green Belt set out in paragraph 143 of the NPPF, 

officers do not consider any would be compromised. The site forms part of the wider park 

experience and functions as an outdoor recreational activity, consistent wit h paragraph 150 of

# 4.2.3  Heritage impact 

Approach to heritage consideration s

4.2.3.1  The beginning of the Agenda Pack sets out the legislative background and the national and 

borough level policy context . Reference should be made to those materials. 

4.2.3.2  The first step is to identify the nature and extent of any harm to the significance of any 

designated heritage assets. This will be a matter for the planning judgement of the decision -

maker, looking at the facts of the application and taking into account the importance of the 

asset in question. Minimal harm to the value of a designated heritage asset should still be 

placed within the category of less than substantial harm. When assessing any application 

which may affect the setting of a heritage asset, the impact of cumulative change may need to 

be considered. 

4.2.3.3  Clear and convincing justification is required by NPPF paragraph 2 13 for any harm to the 

significance of a designated heritage asset. The second step is therefore to balance that harm 

against the public benefits of the scheme, applying the requirements of NPPF paragraph 2 15 

in the case of less than substantial harm , or NPPF paragraph 2 14 in the case of substantial 

harm (or total loss of significance) .

4.2.3.4  A public benefit can be anything that delivers economic, social or environmental objectives, 

which are the three overarching objectives of the planning system as set out in the NPPF. The 

PPG advises that “ public benefits should flow from the proposed development. They should 

be of a nature or scale to be of benefit to the public at large and not just be a private benefit. 

However, benefits do not always have to be visible or accessible to the public in orde r to be 

genuine public benefits, for example, work s to a listed private dwelling which secure its future 

as a designated heritage asset could be a public benefit ”. The degree of weight to attach to 

any particular public benefit is a matter for the decision -maker. 

4.2.3.5  Public benefits can include heritage benefits. If heritage harm and heritage benefit arise in 

relat ion to the same asset, the decision -maker should consider whether the benefit would 

prevent the harm from arising and , if it would, avoid double -counting the heritage benefit when 

identifying the public benefits. If there is harm to one asset but a heritage benefit to a different 

asset, the heritage benefit accruing to one should not be treated as meaning that there is no 

heritage harm to the other .↑ To contents 

4.2.3.6  Importantly, the balancing exercises required by NPPF paragraph s 214 and 2 15 are not 

simple unweighted exercises in which the decision -maker is free to give heritage harm 

whatever degree of weight they wish. In Barnwell Manor the Court of Appeal identified that the 

decision -maker needed to give “considerable importance and weight” to any finding of likely 

harm to a listed building or its setting in order properly to perform the section 66 duty. Sections 

66 and 72 of the Planning (Listed Buildings and Conser vation Areas) Act 1990 amount to a 

statutory presumption against development that would harm the significance of a listed 

building or a conservation area. The presumption can be outweighed, but only if there are 

material considerations (which will include a proposal’s public benefits) that are strong enough 

to do so. 

4.2.3.7  The decision -maker needs to ensure that they give considerable importance and weight to 

harm to the significance of a designated heritage asset, and ensure that the more important 

the asset, the more weight is given to the harm in the balancing exercise . Where the 

significance of more than one designated heritage asset would be harmed by the proposed 

development, the decision -maker needs to account for each individual harm and identify ,

overall, the resulting degree of less than substantial harm or substantial harm. 

4.2.3.8  The assessment of the nature and extent of harm to the significance of a non -designated 

heritage asset is a matter for the planning judgement of the decision -maker, looking at the 

facts of the application and considering the importance of the asset in question. The NPPF 

does not prescribe how the balanced judgement required should be undertaken, or what 

weight should be given to any particular matter.

# 4.3.1  Compliance with the development plan 

4.3.1.1  Section 70(2) of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 states that in dealing with an 

application for planning permission the local planning authority shall have regard to the 

provisions of the development plan, so far as material to the application , a post -examination 

draft neighbourhood development plan, any local finance considerations (as defined in section 

70(4), to include CIL receipts) and to any other material considerations . Section 38(6) of the 

Planning and Compulsory Purchase Act 2004 requires p lanning decisions to be made in 

accordance with the development plan unless material considerations indicate otherwise. 

4.3.1.2  When assessing whether an application complies with the development plan, it is necessary to 

look at the development plan as a whole. There may be policies that pull in different directions. 

It is necessary to reach a judgement about whether, when looked at overall, the application 

amounts to a departure from the development plan . Factors relevant to this judgement can 

include the number of policies not complied with, the extent of any non -compliance , how up to 

date various policies are , and whether a polic y relates to the principle of development or a 

more minor aspect .

Common for a temporary leisure facility comprising two saunas, six plunge pools, a kiosk, and ↑ To contents

operation, and removal of all temporary structures between 1st October 2025 till 30th April 

2026

impact on the openness of Metropolitan Open Land (MOL). All structures are temporary and 

reversible, and the site will be restored to its original condition foll owing the operating period. 

The use does not conflict with other land use policies within the development plan. 

4.3.1.5  Proposed measures for transport, amenity, ecology, trees and biodiversity, flooding and 

surface water, security, transport are considered satisfactory, ensuring compliance with

report and do not conflict with the development pla n with save for those relating to heritage 

where a low degree of less than substantial harm to Clapham Common Conservation area, 

and the Church of Holy Trinity a designated heritage assets has been outlined. 

4.3.1.7  In officers’ assessment, the identified conflict with heritage policies is not such as to mean that 

the development plan, when looked at overall, is not complied with, the public benefits need to 

be considered and are outlined within the report . The application of section 72 and section 66 

imposes a duty to treat harm to a designated heritage asset as a consideration to which the
